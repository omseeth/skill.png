## Require an Exact Result Before Choosing
For theorem-recall questions, write down the result being used with its hypotheses and precise conclusion before matching it to an option. Vague recollections such as “a standard result gives a power law” do not determine an exponent or endpoint. Check that each inference is valid: numerical invariants such as rank do not automatically behave monotonically under inclusion. If the exact result or deduction is not established, do not promote a plausible analogue to a proved claim.

## Audit the Nearest Distractor Literally
Before finalizing, compare the candidate with its closest alternative clause by clause. For formulas, align coefficients, exponents, index ranges, and boundary cases; for logical statements, write the quantifier order; for algorithms, check the exact output and guarantee. Do not call choices equivalent or rank them by apparent strength until these differences are resolved. Universal and existential claims may be incomparable, so choose only a statement whose full content is supported.

## Check Bijections and Exact Near-Choice Differences
A constrained parameterization can still index the entire target set; establish injectivity and surjectivity before calling it a proper subset. For close quantitative alternatives, normalize the formulas and compare exactly: divisibility direction and factors, and \(O(\cdot)\) versus asymptotic equivalence, constants, and exponents.

## Separate Global Existence from Uniform Estimates
Existence for all time, with bounds on each finite interval \([0,T]\), does not imply bounds uniform over all \(T>0\). Treat uniformity in time, parameters, or data as a separate conclusion requiring explicit support.
