### Working method

- First isolate the exact target: what conclusion is being asked for, under which fixed hypotheses, and whether the question asks for a classification, equality, bound, implication, obstruction, existence statement, or “strongest” version.
- Rewrite the hypotheses mentally as a checklist: objects, parameter ranges, exceptional cases, alternative assumptions, and any extra conditions tied to particular cases. Do not import assumptions not stated.
- Treat the five choices as variants of one theorem. Compare them against each other before judging them individually. Most wrong choices differ by one endpoint, missing hypothesis, extra conclusion, reversed implication, wrong exponent, wrong constant, or unjustified strengthening.

### Finding the strongest true choice

- Separate each option into:
  - hypotheses it requires,
  - conclusions it gives,
  - parameter ranges or exceptional cases,
  - exact formulas/constants if present.
- A statement is stronger if it has weaker hypotheses, stronger conclusions, sharper quantitative bounds, more complete case coverage, or fewer exceptions. But the correct answer is the strongest statement that actually holds, not merely the most ambitious option.
- If two options share a base conclusion and one adds an extra property, decide whether that extra property is a standard consequence of the named theorem or an overreach.
- If an option weakens a hypothesis from the question or changes the range of a parameter, ask whether the theorem plausibly remains valid in that larger range. Endpoint changes are common traps.
- If an option adds a conditional “moreover” clause, test whether the added condition is sufficient for the added conclusion, or whether it is too weak/too strong compared with the natural theorem.

### Cheap falsification tests

Use quick checks to eliminate overstrong or malformed choices.

- Boundary cases: smallest allowed dimension, rank, genus, weight, prime, exponent, or index; endpoints such as \(0,1,N,N-1\); equality cases; empty or degenerate alternatives.
- Quantifiers: distinguish “there exists one,” “for all,” “for sufficiently large,” “uniform in all parameters,” and “for the same parameter.” A theorem requiring the same auxiliary object is not implied by existence of some unrelated one.
- Direction: verify which map/image/subset/inclusion is being asserted. Purely formal inclusions may be obvious in one direction and false in the other.
- Strength of properties: names with prefixes such as “fully,” “strongly,” “uniformly,” “generically,” “separates,” “finite,” “injective,” or “onto” are not interchangeable.
- Quantitative scale: check dimensions, powers, signs, logarithms, normalization, and whether a bound is additive or multiplicative. A stronger asymptotic factor may be impossible even if a weaker one is known.
- Global/local products or case formulas: align the common parts and inspect only the differing factors, ranges, signs, exponents, and exceptional clauses. Test the boundary values where cases meet.

### Handling formula-heavy choices

- Do not rederive long formulas from scratch. Make a comparison table of the differing tokens: case ranges, equality/inequality conditions, signs, powers, shifted indices, denominators, and auxiliary constants.
- Prefer the option whose cases are exhaustive, mutually consistent, and respect the definitions given in the question.
- Endpoint consistency is especially informative: if a symbol is defined to allow an endpoint, the correct case range usually either includes it with an explicit exception or sends it to the “otherwise” case for a reason.
- Check that expressions are well-defined in the stated cases: no division by a quantity that may vanish, no undefined valuation endpoint, no symbol used outside its declared range.
- A vague option is usually weaker than an otherwise identical precise complete formula, unless all precise formulas contain a detectable error.

### Using mathematical memory safely

- If you recognize the theorem, match the exact hypotheses and conclusion rather than a nearby result. Recent-paper questions often test a subtle strengthening, endpoint, or auxiliary condition.
- If memory is partial, use structural plausibility: standard theorems rarely prove a strictly stronger statement after removing a named necessary hypothesis; they often include technical conditions for small primes, singular cases, inseparability, ramification, compactness, or residual image.
- Avoid proving a different classical theorem. The choices are designed around the cited result; the correct answer often matches the precise theorem statement.

### Bounded reasoning discipline

- Spend most effort on differences among options, not on common text.
- After eliminating options with clear counterchecks, choose among the remaining by exact fit to hypotheses and strength.
- Do not continue searching for remote objections once one option is the unique plausible strongest true statement and the others each have a concrete flaw.
- Keep internal derivations concise enough to leave room for the required final response.
